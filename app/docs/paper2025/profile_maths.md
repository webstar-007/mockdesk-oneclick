## 2. Summary

- Mensuration: 7 (solids 6: Q2, 6, 7, 11, 22, 24; plane Q30). The largest block, about 23%.
- Statistics / data: 6 (Q1, 8, 16, 19, 26, 27).
- Arithmetic: 5 (Q10 CI, Q12 ratio, Q15 partnership, Q28 time and work, Q29 ratio). No percentage, profit-loss, speed, mixture or SI questions appear in this set of 30.
- Geometry: 4 (Q4, 18, 20, 21).
- Trigonometry: 3 (Q9, 14, 25), all identity or algebraic manipulation, no heights and distances.
- Algebra: 2 (Q13, Q17).
- Simplification, number system, probability: 1 each (Q5, Q23, Q3).

DI content: only 2 true table questions (Q19 grouped mean with 7 rows, Q27 grouped median with 5 rows) plus 2 small raw-data lists (Q1, Q8) and 2 mode/spread conceptual items (Q16, Q26). There are no bar, pie or line graphs. Tables are short and every row is needed.

Number sizes: mostly small and "clean". Radius 21 and 2.1 (pi = 22/7), sides 10/13/20, ratios with single digits, totals 33,000 / 4,800 / 297,000. The only large numbers are CI 15,625 -> 19,683 (perfect cubes, which must be recognised), and 5-digit money values. Decimals appear only in Q5 (4-digit recurring approximations) and in the approximate-answer questions (Q11, Q22, Q27). Surds appear in Q6, 9, 17, 22, 24, 25.

Steps: average about 3. Easy items (1-2 steps): Q6, 13, 16, 20, 26, 29, 30. Hard items need 4-5 steps: Q7 (5), Q14 (4-5), Q15 (5), Q28 (5), Q11, Q12, Q18, Q19, Q24, Q27 (4 each).

Lengthy: Q1 and Q8 (multi-part answers), Q15 and Q28 (long word problems with two phases), Q19 (7-row table arithmetic), Q7 (five computations). About 6 of 30.

Hard (4): Q14 (long identity chain with a hidden perfect square in the denominator), Q15 (time-weighted capital with a 4/8 month split and fractional changes), Q28 (replacement mid-way, then asked for 60% of the work rather than the whole job). No question reaches 5. Average difficulty is about 2.4 (roughly 8 questions at 1, 10 at 2, 9 at 3, 3 at 4). A candidate left Q4, Q10, Q14 and Q23 blank, so these four (Q14 hardest, then Q4 and Q10 geometry/CI traps, Q23 unusual concept) are probably the ones perceived as hardest in practice.

What makes it harder than a textbook:
- Hidden setup: the quantity is not given directly (Q7 height from volume, Q12 fifth term from a condition, Q24 edges from face diagonals, Q4 with no figure).
- Final ask differs from the natural result (Q10 half-yearly vs annual, Q28 60% of work, Q11 half the tank and conversion to minutes, Q2 flat faces).
- Composite shapes and pieces (cut sphere, trapezium prism).
- Concept questions that need a single non-obvious fact (Q23, Q18, Q21, Q16).
- Identity recognition in trig and algebra (Q14, Q9, Q25, Q17).
- Red herrings (Q30 perimeter), approximate-answer items (Q5, Q22).

Option style: 4 numeric options, no "none of these". Distractors are structural, each corresponding to a likely mistake (half or double the answer, wrong rate period, forgetting a surface or step). Numerically close sets exist (Q2: 450.75 / 551.25 / 560 / 625.25 pi; Q22: 683 / 782 / 982 / 1092; Q12 and Q27 moderately close). Fractions in Q3, Q18, Q25. Ratios in Q21. Surd forms in Q6, 17. Multi-part answers (Q1, Q8) where options differ by only one component, so every part must be computed.

## 3. Rules for matching this paper's difficulty

1. About 60% of questions should be answerable in 1-3 quick steps (difficulty 1-2); about 30% need 3-5 steps; about 10% reach difficulty 4.
2. Every non-trivial question must hide one setup step (derive a missing dimension, term or rate) before the main formula applies.
3. Use clean, small numbers that give neat answers (pi = 22/7 with radius 21 or 2.1, side lengths from Pythagorean triples, totals divisible by the ratio sum). Allow one "recognise the perfect power" number pair per paper (like 15625 and 19683).
4. Build each distractor from a specific, plausible error (half/double, wrong time unit, missing surface, wrong centre), not random values.
5. The question must end with a changed target (annual not half-yearly, a percentage of the whole work, litres to minutes, the partial surface) so that the intermediate result is not the answer.
6. Mensuration should be the heaviest chapter (about 20-25%): include at least one cut or composite solid, one prism or pyramid with a derived dimension, and one unit conversion.
7. Include about 5-6 statistics items: at least one grouped mean (6-7 classes) and one grouped median (5 classes), plus a few raw-data or property items. Tables stay small (5-7 rows) and no graphs are needed.
8. Trig and algebra items should rely on identities (complementary angles, (tan+cot)^2, cube identities with surds, ratio substitution) rather than values at standard angles only.
9. Geometry should test centres, perpendicular bisector or circle-angle facts in short statements, with no figure supplied.
10. Include 1-2 pure-concept questions (centre coincidences, divisor count, SD property) and 1-2 red-herring items with unused data; keep multi-part answers where all parts must be checked (mode / above-average count).

Five ways to be slightly harder:
1. Chain two setups in one problem (e.g. a prism whose base needs a derived height AND an unstated unit conversion).
2. Make the time-and-work or partnership question have three phases or two changed members, asking for a fraction or percentage of the work or profit.
3. Use a mixed grouped table with unequal class widths or a missing frequency to be found from the mean or median, before computing the requested value.
4. Require an identity with a two-step simplification (e.g. cube or fourth-power sums with surds, or trig expressions needing both (sin+cos)^2 and a ratio).
5. Make distractors numerically close (within about 5%) and add approximation (pi = 22/7, 3 decimals) so that sloppy rounding or a skipped step lands on a wrong option.
