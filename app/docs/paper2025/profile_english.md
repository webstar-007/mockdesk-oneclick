Roughly 20 of 45 questions are comprehension or cloze. 25 are grammar or vocabulary stand-alone items, with transformations (narration and voice) making up 7 of those.

### Comprehension passages
- **P1 (Q26-30):** a literary study of Pride and Prejudice. About 230 words in 5 short paragraphs. Themes: pride and prejudice as character flaws, satire of class, marriages of love versus convenience. Question styles: theme, defect named in the title, character function, turning-point event, author's valued marriage type. Mostly retrieval and inference. No vocabulary-in-context question.
- **P2 (Q31-35):** current-affairs policy, about a rural employment guarantee act replacing an earlier one. About 160-170 words in 2 paragraphs. It is dense with proper nouns and statistics. Question styles: author's purpose, tone, inference, funding-gap significance, "sustainable development" in context.
- **P3 (Q36-40):** a banking-supervision argument built on two bank-failure case studies. About 170 words in 4 short paragraphs. Question styles: logic of the conclusion, why an example is used (stress tests), cause of slow supervision, meaning of "positive incentives", tone.
- Overall: 3 passages x 5 questions, 160-230 words each. Four of 15 questions are purpose/tone, about 4 are inference, and the rest are retrieval/paraphrase. The options are paraphrased, not quoted.

### Cloze
- One passage of about 90 words with 5 blanks (about 2 per 36 words). Topic: the rupee's moves against the dollar and its tie to crude oil.
- Blank types: preposition, verb (recovered), participle (reached), "tied to", verb in the perfect (strengthened).
- Distractors for 3 of 5 blanks are other parts of speech, so a correct sentence-shape guess is enough. Only blank 1 (against) needs context.

- Synonym: erroneous to fallacious.
- Antonym: gullibility to skepticism (distractors innocence, naivety, trust).

- Spelling: impetuous (deformed), grotesque.

### Grammar rules tested
- Reported speech: yes/no questions (whether/if), imperatives (order X to), "said" versus "asked", pronoun and tense shift, vocative handling.
- Voice: past continuous passive to active, wh-question passive, present perfect passive with two sentences.
- Fixed phrases and word choice: "peals of laughter", "beside" versus "besides", "if you will catch" versus "want to", "invest another sum" versus "more".
- Phrasal verb: give off.
- Para-jumble cues: contrast markers (however), opening general claims, pronoun/definite-article back-reference, example sentences closing.

### Distractor quality
- Mostly easy-to-moderate. About 25 questions have at least two distractors that are clearly wrong by word class, tense or meaning.
- The hardest distractors are in the para jumbles (Q15), RC logic/inference (Q36, Q37), narration (Q13) and antonym with near-synonym decoys (Q7).
- RC distractors are paraphrase-length and plausible mainly through extreme words ("certainly", "purely", "necessarily").
- Several RC and cloze options are written in simple, slightly stilted English.

## 3. Rules and escalation

### Ten rules for matching this paper
1. Keep the mix: about 15 RC (3 passages x 5), 5 cloze, 7 transformation items, 4 para jumbles, 2 error-spotting, 2 sentence-improvement, 2 idioms, 2 spelling, 5 vocabulary items.
2. RC passages 160-230 words, 2-5 short paragraphs. Use one literary, one current-affairs/policy and one economics/finance topic.
3. RC questions: roughly 40% direct retrieval/paraphrase, 40% inference/purpose/tone, 20% vocabulary-in-context or structural logic. Options are full sentences of similar length.
4. RC distractors: each wrong option is one of (a) an extreme version of the truth, (b) reversed cause and effect, (c) outside knowledge not in the passage, (d) a true fact answering a different question.
5. Cloze: 5 blanks in about 90-110 words on a current-affairs topic. Mix 1 preposition, 1-2 verb forms, 1 collocation and 1 adverb or participle. A couple of distractors can be wrong word classes.
6. Narration and voice: pick one tense/aspect trap per question (past continuous passive, present perfect, a question inversion), and make wrong options differ in exactly one feature.
7. Error spotting and sentence improvement: put the error in a fixed phrase or word choice, not in obvious subject-verb agreement, and make the "no error" or "no improvement" option a live distractor at least once.
8. Para jumbles: 4 sentences with one opener that is not obviously first, plus contrast and pronoun cues. Parts-style jumbles are a single sentence split into 4 fragments with strict grammatical continuity.
9. Vocabulary: one word per item at graduate-reader level, with distractors that are near-synonyms or reversals. Include one rare coinage (a phobia term or "-ology" word) and one proverb or biblical idiom.
10. Mix easy and hard: about 40% rated 2, 45% rated 3, 10% rated 4 and 5% rated 1 or 5, so a well-prepared candidate loses time on fewer than 8-10 items.

### Five ways to be slightly harder
1. In RC, make two options partly true so that the candidate must pick the best, not the only true, option. Add one "NOT/EXCEPT" or one author-implied question per passage.
2. Add an unfamiliar vocabulary-in-context item (a repurposed everyday word, e.g. "sojourn" or "pedantry" level) inside the passage and test it.
3. In cloze, make at least two blanks pass grammatical agreement across all options, so that only collocation or meaning decides, and use a longer passage of about 120-150 words.
4. In para jumbles, make two adjacent pairs plausible in more than one order, and ensure the opener is a sentence with a pronoun or connector that looks like it needs a predecessor.
5. In transformations, combine two features (a tense shift and a modal, or a wh-question inside a passive) and make distractors differ by a single subtle word (for example "had been" versus "has been", or "if" versus "whether" with a changed pronoun).
